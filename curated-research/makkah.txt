ELIGIBILITY, CHECK THIS BEFORE ANYTHING ELSE:
Makkah is closed to non-Muslims. This is a legal restriction on entry to the city itself, not merely to the mosque, and it is enforced at checkpoints on the approach roads. An itinerary for Makkah is only valid for Muslim travellers. If the customer's faith has not been established anywhere in their submission, that is a genuine "needs the customer's input" item and must be raised before any Makkah plan is built or priced, not assumed. This is the single highest-consequence fact about this city.

Restaurants:
Budget tier:
Al Baik - Saudi Arabia's best-known fast-food chain, extremely popular with pilgrims. The branch on Ajyad Street opposite Hilton Suites is the closest to the Haram. A full meal runs roughly SAR 15-25. Reliable, fast, and a safe recommendation for almost any group.
Ajyad Street stalls - street-level food stalls in the Ajyad area, meals from roughly SAR 10-15, the most budget-friendly option near the Haram.
Misfalah and Aziziah district canteens - budget canteens serving generous, filling meals to pilgrims on tight budgets. Good value, plain surroundings.
Abraj Al Bait (Clock Tower) food courts - a wide range of options in one place, convenient for families who cannot agree, and directly adjacent to the Haram.
Tuheed Pakistani Restaurant - in the Clock Tower complex, hearty curries and biryani.
Mid tier:
Al Shorfa - near the Clock Tower, Middle Eastern, noted for a more pleasant setting than the canteen options.
Romansiah - authentic Saudi, mandi and kabsa platters sized to feed two or three people, which makes it strong value for a family rather than a per-head expense.
Simple rice or chicken dishes across the mid-tier run roughly SAR 15-30 per person.
Range note: Ibrahim Al Khalil Street, the main artery connecting the Haram to the outer hotel district, carries a genuine spread of cuisines - Lebanese grills, Pakistani karahi houses, Turkish pide bakeries, Indonesian and Yemeni places. For an international pilgrim group with mixed tastes this street is the practical answer, and it is worth naming to the customer as such.
Upscale tier: not researched in depth here. Makkah's genuinely upscale dining is concentrated in the five-star hotels ringing the Haram (the Clock Tower complex and similar) rather than in standalone restaurants. If a customer wants fine dining, the team should look at hotel restaurants directly rather than expecting a separate restaurant scene.

Attractions:
Masjid al-Haram & the Kaaba - the central site, open continuously. Crowd levels are the entire planning problem here, not opening hours. Peak congestion around the five daily prayers and dramatically higher during Ramadan and Hajj season. Any itinerary should be built around prayer times rather than ignoring them.
Jabal al-Nour & the Cave of Hira - accessible, but this is a serious physical undertaking and must not be presented casually. The ascent is roughly 1,200 steps and can take up to two hours depending on fitness, heat and crowds. An early-morning start is strongly recommended to avoid both. Trekking shoes and proper trousers are advised. For any group including elderly travellers, young children, or anyone with mobility or cardiac limitations, this should be flagged as optional and physically demanding rather than slotted into a day as a normal stop. Getting this wrong is how a family ends up stranded halfway up a mountain in the heat.
Jabal Thawr - site of the Cave of Thawr, where the Prophet and Abu Bakr sheltered for three nights during the Hijrah. Also a climb, and like Jabal al-Nour it should be treated as an effort-dependent optional rather than a standard stop. Specific current access details and hours were not confirmed in this research.
General: specific ticketing was not found for these sites, they are religious/heritage sites rather than ticketed attractions. Nusuk permits govern Umrah performance itself; confirm current permit requirements at booking time as these have changed repeatedly in recent years.

Rental cars and getting around:
Not researched specifically for Makkah in this pass. Note that driving and parking near the Haram is genuinely difficult and heavily restricted during peak periods, so a rental car is often the wrong recommendation here even when a customer asks for one. The Haramain High-Speed Railway connects Makkah to Jeddah in roughly 1 hour and to Madinah, with a station inside Jeddah airport Terminal 1, and is usually the better answer for intercity movement. Local movement near the Haram is mostly on foot or by short taxi/ride-hail hop.

Flights:
Makkah has no airport. Nearly all arrivals come via Jeddah (JED, King Abdulaziz International), roughly an hour away by Haramain train or by road transfer. Madinah (MED) is the alternative gateway for those starting in Madinah. No flight times or prices researched, the team prices these separately.
