ELIGIBILITY, READ FIRST:
Madinah differs from Makkah and the distinction matters. Since the 2021 reforms non-Muslims are permitted in the city of Madinah generally, but mosque interiors, including Al-Masjid an-Nabawi itself, remain restricted to Muslims. Non-Muslim visitors can access the surrounding courtyards and avenues and view the architecture from outside. Reporting on the exact boundary of the restricted central zone is inconsistent across sources and policy has changed more than once, so for a non-Muslim customer this must be verified against current official guidance at booking time rather than assumed from this note. Do not tell a non-Muslim customer they can enter the Prophet's Mosque. Do not tell them Madinah is closed to them either, as that is no longer accurate.

Restaurants:
Budget tier:
Al Baik - the well-known Saudi fried chicken chain, within roughly 1-2 km of Masjid an-Nabawi. Cheap, fast, reliably popular.
Gate 330 area - the numbered northern-perimeter gate of Masjid an-Nabawi has become known for affordable, good-quality street food. A useful, specific landmark to give a customer rather than a vague "street food near the mosque".
Beiruti - casual Lebanese, shawarma, manakish and falafel, roughly SAR 20-50 per person, close to the mosque.
Mid tier:
Zaitoon - short walk from the Haram, Pakistani and Middle Eastern, meals around SAR 50 with generous portions. Staff reported as fluent in English and Urdu and the setting is family-friendly, which makes it a strong pick for international pilgrim families who may struggle with language elsewhere.
Swiss House - cafe-style dishes, roughly SAR 30-50 per person.
Al Tazaj and Kudu - familiar regional chains, dependable mid-range, good when a group wants something predictable.
Mid-range dining in Madinah broadly sits in the SAR 25-60 per person band.
Upscale tier: not researched in depth. As with Makkah, the higher end is concentrated in the hotels around the Haram rather than in standalone restaurants; direct the team to hotel dining if a customer wants that.
Madinah is also known for its dates, which are a genuine local specialty and an easy, well-received addition to an itinerary or as a gift stop.

Attractions:
Al-Masjid an-Nabawi (the Prophet's Mosque) - the central site, open continuously, with the same prayer-time crowd dynamics as Makkah. The Rawdah area requires a separate booked permit through the Nusuk platform and slots are limited and go quickly; this is a common source of customer disappointment and should be flagged as something to arrange early rather than on arrival. Confirm current Rawdah booking rules at planning time, they change.
Quba Mosque - the first mosque built in Islam, roughly 3.5 km south of the Prophet's Mosque, reachable by a short taxi or ride-hail, or a 30-40 minute walk for those who want it. Interior restricted to Muslims; courtyard accessible more broadly. There is particular merit in visiting Quba after praying at the Prophet's Mosque, which many pilgrims plan around.
Mount Uhud - roughly 4 km north of the Prophet's Mosque, rising about 1,077 m, site of the Battle of Uhud and the associated martyrs' cemetery. Freely accessible outdoor site, no tickets or fixed hours found. Non-Muslim visitors have reported visiting without restriction, but this is not authoritatively confirmed and should be checked rather than promised. Early morning is best for heat.
Specific opening hours were not found for Quba or Uhud, which is consistent with them being religious and open-air heritage sites rather than ticketed attractions. Treat prayer times, not opening hours, as the scheduling constraint in this city.

Rental cars and getting around:
Not researched specifically for Madinah in this pass. The three key sites (Nabawi, Quba, Uhud) all sit within about 4 km of the centre, so short taxi or ride-hail hops generally serve better than a rental car, and parking near the Haram is difficult during peak periods. The Haramain High-Speed Railway connects Madinah to Jeddah in roughly 2 hours and onward to Makkah, and is normally the right intercity choice.

Flights:
MED (Prince Mohammad bin Abdulaziz International) serves Madinah directly and handles substantial international pilgrim traffic, including direct service from several Muslim-majority countries, so many Umrah travellers can fly straight in rather than routing via Jeddah. Saudia, flynas and flyadeal all operate here alongside international carriers. A common and efficient pattern is to fly into MED, travel Madinah to Makkah by Haramain train, and depart from JED, which avoids backtracking. No flight times or prices researched, the team prices these separately.

Typical costs (researched ranges, use as honest guides, never as quotes):
Hotels per night: budget rooms near the Haram from roughly USD 17-30; 3-star from about USD 28; mid-range 4-star roughly USD 78-106, with some properties reported far lower. Higher-end stays average around USD 222.
Seasonal pattern, more useful than any single figure: quieter months (March-May, September-November) run roughly SAR 300-400 per night; Ramadan and Hajj push rates to roughly SAR 500-800; winter (December-February) and late summer (July-August) are cheapest at roughly SAR 250-350. Tell the customer where their dates fall on that curve, it is genuinely actionable.
Eating out: the per-person figures in the restaurants section above (Beiruti SAR 20-50, Zaitoon around SAR 50, Swiss House SAR 30-50, general mid-range SAR 25-60) are the honest guide.
